In one minute

The question changed
twice, and the second
version was the product.

First question

Is hyperlocal worth entering at all?

Second question

Which demand can a small team serve reliably, in one place, first?

What I contributed

The market model, the scope call, and the cross-functional alignment that got it launched.

Outcome

A hyperlocal MVP in production in six months, at 15-minute P80 pickup (80% of pickups inside 15 minutes) and 99.9% uptime across 18,000+ daily orders.

Most of the work was in the second question. A convincing market size is easy to obtain and tells you almost nothing about whether a specific city will fill.

Sizing

Three lenses, because
one number is a story
someone else told you.

I used three independent views of the opportunity: light commercial vehicle purchases, competitor revenue, and earnings per vehicle. Any one of them is a marketing argument. Together they are a check on each other.

Lens What it answers well What it cannot answer alone
LCV purchases Whether vehicle supply is growing Active fleet, utilisation, or share available for on-demand work
Competitor revenue Whether organised demand monetises Total market size. Reported revenue can bundle services and use different accounting
Earnings per vehicle Whether supplying trips is worth a driver's time Platform revenue. Needs utilisation, commissions, idle time, and working days

A market model is only useful if it tells you what has to be true for the product to work.

The bridge from size to scope

The model, in the order it has to run

Supply-side activity

Active vehicles × paid trips per vehicle × realised fare × working days

Serviceable activity

Filtered to launch geography, vehicle type, use case, and supply that is genuinely free

Potential platform revenue

Serviceable booking value × obtainable share × effective revenue rate

Competitor revenue is a cross-check once the service mix and accounting line up. Driver earnings are a viability check after idle time and fuel. Neither is another segment to add to the total, which is the most common way a market model ends up three times too big.

A defensible number separates annual purchases from the operating fleet, gross bookings from platform revenue, and national demand from what one city can absorb. Those are different quantities and they should reconcile rather than add up.

Scope

What is the smallest
product that can fail?

The temptation in 0-to-1 is to build the category. I kept asking a narrower question: what is the least we can ship that still tells us whether anyone wants this?

The answer was a complete journey for one demand type, in a bounded geography, with no automation of operational exceptions. Everything else waited for evidence.

01 / DEMAND

One specific shipping need

Who needs a local trip, what they are moving, and what they use today instead.

02 / SUPPLY

Viable vehicle economics

Whether trip frequency and earnings attract supply in the same place at the same hours.

03 / EXPERIENCE

A whole first journey

Booking, pickup detail, assignment, tracking, completion, and help when it goes wrong.

04 / LEARNING

Visible failure points

Instrument where demand drops out and where the delivery promise breaks.

The boundary I drew

Built into the coreHandled by hand at launchDeferred
Booking, pickup and drop detail, assignment visibility, completionServiceability limits, manual exception escalation, a named support ownerBroad geographic coverage and adjacent trip types
Events that expose conversion and fulfilment failureA fixed weekly review of failed bookings and incomplete tripsAutomating every operational exception

Handing exceptions to a human is the right call at launch and the wrong call forever. What makes it acceptable is that it is explicit and owned, not that it is clever.

Leadership

Making four teams
agree on one promise.

A hyperlocal launch pulls product, engineering, and operations in different directions, and the tension is real rather than rhetorical. A wider service area is more commercially attractive. A narrower one is easier to actually fulfil.

My job was not to pick a winner. It was to make the trade visible enough that everyone could see what they were giving up, and then get an explicit decision rather than a series of quiet vetoes.

Perspective What they need answered Artifact that settles it
Business Which demand is worth pursuing first? Target segment with explicit economics assumptions
Operations Where can the service promise actually hold? Serviceability boundary and a named exception owner
Engineering What makes a first journey complete rather than partial? MVP scope and dependency sequence
Product What evidence justifies expanding? Launch scorecard with written review criteria

The resolution: agree the serviceability boundary first, publish what got deferred and why, name an owner for exceptions, and review expansion against fulfilment and unit economics instead of launch enthusiasm.

Publishing the deferred list is the part that gets skipped. Without it, every team quietly assumes its own out-of-scope item is coming back next quarter, and the roadmap never converges.

Analytical risk

The failure mode
in this market.

There is a specific way this analysis goes wrong, and it is worth naming because the error is common and it looks like good news first.

The claim to challenge: a large pool of vehicles means an on-demand marketplace can reliably fulfil demand.

Why it fails: those vehicles may be fully booked, running captive routes, or idle in a different city from the demand. Supply in the national aggregate and supply available on demand in one launch area are not the same quantity.

How I kept the model honest

  • Separate new vehicle sales from the operating fleet. Account for retirement and captive usage.
  • Reconcile competitor figures to comparable services, periods, and revenue definitions before treating them as a total.
  • Model driver earnings after idle time, empty travel, and running costs, not gross fares.
  • Replace one market-size number with a range and show sensitivity to utilisation, since that assumption dominates everything downstream.
  • Spend the launch testing whichever assumption the decision is most sensitive to, rather than the one that is easiest to instrument.

The launch result is the real test here. A model that survives contact with a live marketplace is worth more than a wide range attached to a confident slide.

What comes next

A launch proves a team
delivered. It does not prove
the product works.

We shipped in six months, and the operational numbers held: 15-minute P80 pickup at 99.9% uptime across 18,000+ daily orders. That is evidence the service works. It is not evidence of product-market fit, retention, or positive contribution margin.

Question Signal worth trusting
Is the need real? Completed bookings and repeat rate by use case, not by cohort size
Can we keep the promise? Assignment success, pickup-time distribution, cancellations, completion
Does supply actually benefit? Earnings per working hour after relevant costs
Is growth sustainable? Contribution per completed trip, including incentives and support

It is worth being honest about the limit of what I verified here. Operational reliability is what I can show. Whether hyperlocal is a durable business at Delhivery's cost structure is a question that needs longer-run contribution data than a launch produces.

The link to the account-care work is direct. Customer growth sits on top of the service experience, and no amount of intelligent follow-up compensates for a promise the network cannot keep.
