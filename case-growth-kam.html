Deciding which accounts
deserve a call, and which

When the population

The brief

Figure out what AI can take off the account-management team without dropping the standard of care on the accounts that matter.

My direction

Lead with coverage, not call volume. Decide whether a conversation is worth having before choosing an agent or writing a word of it.

What I actually did

Wrote the intervention playbooks, defined what each objective is allowed to do, set the reactivation rules, and rewrote the onboarding conversation.

Where it stands

Working documents, two recorded design corrections, and a reproducible policy prototype with 34 passing checks. Deployment is not claimed.

Scope: I own the product framing and the agent behaviour design. Speech recognition, synthesis, and telephony are a partner's stack, and I do not claim them.

The problem

Account managers
have finite hours.

Every hour a key account manager (KAM) spends on a routine check-in is an hour not spent on an onboarding, a service recovery, or a commercially important conversation. The accounts that suffer are the smaller business shippers, who get no proactive attention at all.

That is a coverage problem before it is an AI problem. Widening it means deciding, per account, whether a conversation would actually help. A pitch to a shipper whose last three deliveries failed is worse than no call, because it confirms that we noticed and did not fix anything.

Should we call this account? comes before what should the agent say.

So I started with eligibility, not scripts. The policy gate below is the product. The conversation is downstream of it.

Decisions

Three choices that
changed the design.

Diagnose the account before I pitch it

An order decline means one of three things: an unresolved supply problem, a change in the shipper's own business, or a naturally infrequent ordering pattern. The first one is not a growth opportunity, it is a service failure we have not closed yet.

What I changed: service context is checked before an objective is selected. Known systemic failures route to Operations. Sensitive cases and explicit human requests route to a person. Only an eligible account with no blocker gets a reactivation conversation.

What it cost: fewer automated calls, because most declines are not growth opportunities. The design is also only as good as its inputs. Without incident feeds and contact-consent state, the gate has to refuse to dispatch, and that is the correct failure direction.

Separate objectives, one shared memory

Onboarding, retention, service recovery, and growth want different outcomes and are allowed different things. They share one account history so the next conversation cannot contradict the promise made in the last one.

Target operating model. The prototype implements the reactivation gate only.
Objective Outcome it is for Boundary
Onboarding Understand the first experience and the next real need Ask only for what is actually missing
Retention Find the blocker and agree a next step No autonomous compensation, no cross-sell
Service recovery Close the issue and restore trust Sensitive cases stay with a person
Growth Explore one relevant additional use case Never outranks an unresolved service problem

What it cost: more coordination, since four objectives now share state. I would not start with all four. Start bounded, share the state, expand when the evaluation supports it.

Build the decision layer, buy the voice stack

Targeting, account context, playbooks, and evaluation encode what we know about a shipper. That knowledge is ours and it is the product. Turning text into a phone call is a solved problem with a price tag.

What I decided: keep the intervention logic and rent the voice capability. The open work is the unglamorous part of that boundary: what happens when the partner call fails, who reads the transcript, and what latency we are willing to accept before a shipper notices.

System design

Permission lives in code
the model cannot edit.

The language model is good at summarising context and holding a conversation. It should not be the thing that decides whether it is allowed to call, issue compensation, or overwrite a customer record.

01 / CONTEXT

Grounded account brief

Observed behaviour, prior commitments, current service state, and an explicit list of what is not known.

02 / POLICY

Inspectable gate

Eligibility, contact limits, incident routing, human ownership, holdout assignment.

03 / CONVERSATION

One bounded objective

A model-assisted agenda and call, restricted to the actions this account permits.

04 / EXECUTION

Validate before writing

Re-check eligibility and caps, reject replayed actions, record the result, keep an audit trail.

The distinction that matters: screening an input for risk runs alongside generation, because the risk is in what came in. Checking a generated answer has to wait for the answer. An evaluator that never saw the output cannot certify the output is safe, whatever score it reports.

The prototype runs the deterministic gate and assembles a template brief. It does not call a model, contact a shipper, or write to an account record.

Corrections

Two times I had to
throw out my own analysis.

The timing model was selected on the outcome

My first inactivity analysis measured the gaps between orders among shippers who came back. That excluded every non-returner, and it made long waits look reasonable. I had picked the population on the exact outcome I was trying to move.

Including non-returners helped, and then made it worse: the revised thresholds were calling infrequent shippers earlier than frequent ones, which is the opposite of the intended cadence logic.

Where I started

Order gaps among returning shippers, to estimate when to call.

Why it failed

Non-returners were excluded. The sample was selected on the outcome under test.

What broke next

With them included, thresholds were backwards. Infrequent shippers got called before frequent ones.

Where it landed

Intervene relative to demonstrated habit. Treat every threshold as a hypothesis until it survives a backtest and a controlled pilot.

What actually changed was my standard of evidence, not a retention number. The timing rule is different and the bar for believing it is higher.

The voice was re-asking things it already knew

Onboarding calls kept confirming information the shipper had just given. A typical turn: the shipper says they send thirty to forty deliveries a day, and the agent spends another beat checking whether that was understood correctly.

The problem is not one wasted turn. It is that the shipper learns the conversation is not listening, and then starts cooperating less with the rest of it.

Behaviour I was pushing back on

So, thirty to forty deliveries. Did I understand that correctly?

Validating an answer that was already usable.

What the revision looks like

Which vehicle do you usually use?

Capture the range silently. Move to the next missing question, or close if there isn't one.

Illustrative of the correction I documented, not a transcript. Vehicle preference is skipped when it is already known.

Acceptance criteria I set: no reopened questions, no second introduction, no invented precision, no repeated request for permission after a clear answer. These need conversation evaluation, which the routing tests in the lab do not cover.

Coverage means the whole base, not the validation set

The same pass caught a coverage estimate that divided by the validation subset rather than the full population. Accuracy on the eligible subset and reach across the whole base are different questions, and a release decision needs both answered.

Evaluation

Proving the call
changed something

Most of the accounts I would call were going to reorder anyway. Report gross reorder rate and I take credit for the season. The design has to be able to separate the two before anyone is contacted.

Layer What I measure What it establishes
Policy Expected route on fixed scenarios Whether the implemented rules respect the boundaries they were written against
Conversation Unsupported claims, repeated questions, extraction accuracy, handoff completion Whether the voice experience holds up. Not covered by this prototype
Business Reorder rate against a randomised holdout, over a predeclared window Incremental effect, with confidence intervals and cohort cuts
Economics Voice, model, retry, incentive, and review cost per incremental reorder Whether the extra coverage is worth paying for

Rollout, with exits

  1. Offline. Gate, data freshness, and failure handling against agreed scenarios.
  2. Human-assisted. KAMs review briefs and log rejection reasons. This validates the workflow. It is not proof of low risk.
  3. Controlled pilot. One use case, one holdout, reliable contact state, escalation capacity that is actually staffed.
  4. Expand. Only after incremental value and guardrails are reviewed. Autonomous dispatch stops on the first critical permission breach.

No retention lift, model accuracy, or latency figure in this case comes from a production system.

The prototype

Change one fact.
Watch the decision move.

Take the same quiet account. Add a supply incident, a missing consent flag, a recent reorder, or a named human owner. The correct next action changes every time, and you can see which rule did it.
