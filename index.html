The Story








My background is CS, but I ended up in product because the hardest problems I found weren't technical — they were the ones where you'd built the right system and people still didn't use it the way you expected.


At Delhivery, I own intracity: the algorithms that match drivers to orders, the surge pricing that adjusts in real time, and the acceptance mechanics that determine whether a driver takes a trip or waits for something better. It's a marketplace where changing one parameter affects pickup times across six cities within the hour. I've shipped things that worked, and a few that didn't.


In 2024, I started building alongside AI coding agents — Kiro, Cursor, and Claude Code. What began as prototyping driver allocation flows turned into a meaningful workflow: writing specs, generating testable code, reviewing with engineers, shipping faster. I now treat agentic coding as a PM skill, not just a developer tool. It's changed how I scope, how I communicate with eng, and how quickly I can validate assumptions.


Before Delhivery, at Urbanladder, I built UrbanInteriors from zero. The brief was vague: make interior design a business. I spent the first month manually matching customers to designers on WhatsApp before writing a single line of product spec. That manual phase is still the best product research I've done.


My MBA thesis looked at why digital payment adoption stalled in certain segments despite good UX. The answer wasn't UX — it was incentive structure and reference price framing. I use that lens constantly.




"When a metric isn't moving, the first question isn't what's broken — it's what does the user think this costs them."

01 / THE PROBLEM I KEEP SOLVING

The ability to act
is a separate design decision
from the ability to generate.




Once an AI system can take an action a user will notice, a probabilistic output becomes a privileged action. That means the permission to act cannot come from the same reasoning that generated the action.




For tools and writes in particular, the constraint has to be non-bypassable: the model proposes, but a deterministic gate decides. Anything inspecting a produced artifact also needs to see the artifact itself.




01 / MODEL

Interprets, never decides


Summarises context, reads state, drafts an agenda. It produces a proposal, not permission to act.




02 / POLICY

The gate holds the authority


Eligibility, consent, caps, and ownership are enforced by code the model cannot edit or override.




03 / EVALUATOR

Inspects the actual output


It must run on what was produced. Screening the input can run in parallel; certifying a generated artifact cannot.




04 / EXECUTION

Revalidate, then record


Re-check freshness and state at action time. The trace goes to an audit log, not back into the generator's prompt.

03 / THE PROOF STANDARD

How I decide
whether it worked.




Anyone can claim an AI feature worked. The claim is cheap. What is expensive is designing the measurement before you need it, and admitting when your own analysis was the problem.






01

A reorder is not a result unless you kept a holdout.


Most of the accounts an agent would reach were going to return on their own. Report gross reorder rate and I have credited myself for the season. So eligible accounts get randomised before anyone is contacted, and the shippers who do not answer stay inside the treatment group rather than quietly disappearing from the denominator. The number I report is the difference I caused, with the interval attached.
The measurement plan ↗



02

I wrote the rules and the tests, which is weaker than it looks.


My policy prototype passes 34 checks, and I would rather flag that than let a reader assume it is an independent benchmark. Every expected outcome is visible in the fixtures because I wrote both sides of the comparison. Passing means the implementation agrees with my specification. It says nothing about whether the specification is right, and no language model is invoked at all.
Run the checks ↗



03

Twice, the analysis was the bug.


My first retention model only looked at accounts that came back, which selected the sample on the exact outcome I was trying to move and made long gaps look reasonable. Fixing that made it worse: the new thresholds would have contacted infrequent accounts before frequent ones. I had to accept that my competence did not transfer from a previous domain, and rebuild the logic from the definition of the metric up.
Both corrections ↗
